#### CoRGI in MaNGA - II. NGC 5717: cold-gas accretion and disk growth in a BGG
 - **Authors:** Damir Gasymov, Ivan Yu. Katkov, Eva K. Grebel, Anna Pasquali, Evgenii V. Rubtsov, Katarina Kraljic, Igor V. Chilingarian, Kirill A. Grishin, Anna S. Saburova
 - **Subjects:** Subjects:
Astrophysics of Galaxies (astro-ph.GA)
 - **Arxiv link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20704

 - **Pdf link:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20704

 - **Abstract**
 Large-scale stellar counter-rotation, in which two coplanar stellar components rotate in opposite directions, is usually interpreted as a signature of external gas accretion. Such systems are most often found in relatively sparse environments, where cold gas can be acquired and remain dynamically undisturbed long enough to settle into a disk. It is less clear whether this channel can operate in massive central galaxies, whose group or cluster environments can heat, strip, or perturb cold gas. Here we present NGC 5717, one of the most massive galaxies known to host a counter-rotating stellar disk and the first identified case in a brightest group galaxy. Using integral-field spectroscopy from SDSS-IV Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA), together with CFHT imaging, ancillary HI data, and SDSS/DESI spectroscopy of the surrounding group, we show that the galaxy contains a dynamically cold counter-rotating disk embedded in an old dynamically hot elliptical component. The disk is young ($\sim 1.3$ Gyr), metal-poor ([Fe/H] $\sim -0.7$ dex), extended ($h \sim 2.4$ kpc), and massive ($\log M_\star/M_\odot \sim 9.9$); the associated ionized gas co-rotates with it, while the available HI data indicate no significant remaining reservoir of neutral atomic gas ($\log M_{\mathrm{HI}}/M_\odot \lesssim 9$). These properties support an in situ origin of the secondary component from externally accreted gas. The chemical evolution model favors gas accretion from a cosmic filament, which is consistent with the surrounding cosmic-web geometry, and indicates highly efficient burst-like star formation with substantial metal-enriched outflows. NGC 5717 may therefore represent a rare example of disk growth in a massive central galaxy, enabled by external cold-gas accretion during an earlier phase of the group assembly history.
#### How Little Red Dots Turn Blue: Evolution of Kelvin-Helmholtz Contracting Black Hole Envelopes and Observational Diagnostics
 - **Authors:** Zihao Wang, Kohei Inayoshi
 - **Subjects:** Subjects:

 - **Arxiv link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20709

 - **Pdf link:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20709

 - **Abstract**
 James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations have revealed compact, red-colored, broad-line emitters known as little red dots (LRDs), while a bluer counterpart population has been referred to as little blue dots (LBDs). Their physical connection remains unclear: they may represent different evolutionary stages of the same phenomenon, distinct populations, or different phases of the broader evolution of early galaxies and their black holes (BHs). We propose a model in which a rapidly growing BH is embedded in a geometrically thick gaseous envelope accompanied by concurrent nuclear star formation. The model reproduces the observed spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of LRDs and LBDs, and naturally connects the two phases through Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction of the envelope and exposure of the nuclear accretion disk, as the gas supply declines due to external stellar activity or feedback. We predict the corresponding evolutionary tracks in the UV-optical slope ($\beta_{\rm UV}$-$\beta_{\rm opt}$) plane which bridge the apparent gap between LRDs and LBDs. These evolutionary tracks start from the LRD regime and consistently exhibit a red excursion that extends toward $(\beta_{\rm UV},\beta_{\rm opt})\sim(2,-1)$ before turning toward the LBD regime. This feature is largely insensitive to variations in the model parameters that govern the envelope structure, providing a robust signature of the transitional population. The transition lasts $\sim5$-$50\,\mathrm{Myr}$, depending primarily on the BH feedback efficiency and the gas-retention efficiency of the envelope. The abundance ratio of transitional systems to LRDs therefore provides a constraint on BH feedback and gas retention during the rapid growth of early supermassive BHs.

Recent ALMA detections of polarised dust emission indicate that galactic-scale ordered magnetic fields, spanning up to kiloparsec scales, were already present in massive star-forming galaxies at redshifts up to $z\sim5.6$.  - **Abstract**
 We report the identification of a UV attenuation bump in the Black Pearl, a galaxy at a spectroscopic redshift of 9.3335 observed with JWST/NIRSpec as part of the ZENITH and EMBER surveys. This is the highest-redshift UV bump reported to date, probing dust properties only approximately 500 Myr after the Big Bang. The feature is directly detected as a broad depression in the rest-frame UV continuum and is well described by a local Drude profile, with a central wavelength of 2132+/-17 Angstroms, an FWHM of 413+/-54 Angstroms, and a peak attenuation of 0.21+/-0.02 mag.  - **Abstract**
 Pulsars are exceptionally stable clocks, and a compact dark-matter object passing near the line of sight to a pulsar delays its pulses through the Shapiro effect. The number of detectable transits is set by the integral over the dark-matter density along the line of sight connecting the Earth and the pulsar. Established pulsars in pulsar timing arrays are typically within one kiloparsec of the Earth and hence sit in a dark-matter density comparable to our local value. In this work, we explore globular clusters in the inner Galaxy as targets to search for compact dark matter. These high-pulsar-density environments can be cheaply observed with a single telescope beam and enjoy a large dark-matter crossing rate. Additionally, since the recently detected nanohertz gravitational-wave background is nearly common to the cluster's pulsars, a multi-pulsar analysis can remove much of its contribution. Ranking known clusters, we find that Terzan 5, with 49 pulsars, offers the best reach. A forty-year campaign of weekly four-hour visits at the sensitivity envisioned for the second phase of the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) would probe compact objects making up $6\%$ of the dark matter at $0.02\,M_\odot$. Finally, we estimate the foreground from stars and stellar remnants in Terzan 5 and along the sightline. It is subdominant near the most sensitive low-mass part of the SKA benchmark, but becomes important over parts of the stellar mass range.

 - **Abstract**
 Bars are stellar structures commonly found in disk galaxies and important agents of secular evolution. Quantifying the stellar mass content of bars provides a physically motivated approach to assess how bar growth depends on galaxy mass and internal structure. We perform a GALFITM multi-band mid-infrared two-dimensional decomposition of 344 barred galaxies from the Spitzer Survey of Stellar Structure in Galaxies ($S^4G$) in the 3.6 and 4.5 $\mu$m bands. We estimate the stellar mass associated with each structural component (i.e., disk, bulge, bar, and nuclear point source) and investigate how bar properties correlate with global galaxy characteristics. Our final models consist of 2-4 components per galaxy, with the simplest configuration (disk + bar) typically associated with low-mass systems. We find that more massive galaxies with prominent bulges host more massive bars and higher median bar-to-total stellar mass ratios. These bulges generally present Sérsic indices below 2, consistent with a photometric signature of nuclear stellar disks. Additionally, when we consider the relative bar lengths at a fixed galaxy mass, we find a bimodal distribution where galaxies with bulges host relatively longer bars compared to their bulgeless counterparts. These findings could be the result of different evolutionary paths, possibly linked to different disk formation epochs, allowing bars more time to grow in size and mass. Our study indicates that bar evolution may follow distinct pathways across different galaxy mass regimes, and is likely influenced by secular processes and environmental effects.

 - **Abstract**
 Interstellar dust reprocesses approximately half of the starlight emitted across cosmic history. However, dust temperature, which is essential to convert continuum flux into dust mass, molecular gas mass, and obscured star-formation rates, remains largely unconstrained on sub-galactic scales at high redshift. While single-band ALMA imaging has characterized continuum sizes for hundreds of z>1 galaxies, single-frequency measurements cannot separate the dust mass column from the local dust heating. To date, spatially resolved (<0.5-arcsec) multi-band dust temperatures exist in the literature for only ~10 extreme systems (quasars, hyperluminous starbursts, or strongly lensed galaxies) at z>1, with ongoing programs within a few years adding at most a few dozen submillimeter or UV-bright galaxies.  - **Abstract**
 Venus and Mars are unique laboratories for understanding the evolution and diversity of terrestrial planets. Despite their broadly similar rocky compositions, their atmospheres, climates, and surface environments have evolved into dramatically different states. Understanding the physical and chemical processes responsible for these divergent pathways is essential for interpreting terrestrial exoplanets and their habitability. By the 2040s, spacecraft missions will provide unprecedented close-up observations of Venus and Mars, while ground-based facilities can provide the long-term monitoring needed to place these snapshots in the context of atmospheric variability and planetary-scale circulation.  - **Abstract**
 Our understanding of complex organic chemistry during the formation of stars and planets is shaped by a handful of cherry-picked sources with bright molecular emission lines. This observational bias impedes our assessment of how universal chemical complexity is during star formation. The lack of systematic wideband spectral surveys for statistically significant samples of protostars and cores is a major roadblock to properly probing how COM inventories and abundances depend on the vast range of physical conditions, local cloud and global galactic environments, and evolutionary timescales of low- and high-mass star formation. In turn, this limits our ability to constrain and test physicochemical models, which depend critically on these parameters. The next-generation ALMA2040, with a 10x improvement in line sensitivity, is required to observe COMs, that we perceive as common today, in a fraction of the observing time that is needed today with ALMA. Such capabilities will propel us into the era of chemical demographics, whereby wideband (>100 GHz) spectral surveys at high sensitivity (routinely probing COM abundances down to 0.01% relative to CH3OH) will be executed towards >1000-10000 prestellar and protostellar sources. With robust chemical inventories towards large source sample sizes, we will be able to probe for dependencies of chemical complexity on mass regime, feedback processes in a cluster, Galactic position, metallicity, and time-variable accretion. Only with statistically significant samples at high sensitivity will we be able to assess the ubiquity and homogeneity of COM chemistry across the protostellar mass spectrum and natal cores. Constraining the diversity and availability of such species is critical for our understanding of the chemical reservoir available for planet formation, which eventually shapes (exo)planetary atmosphere compositions.
